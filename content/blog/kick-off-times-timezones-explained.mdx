# Kick-Off Times Explained: How to Read a Fixture List in Your Own Timezone

Two websites, one fixture, two different kick-off times. Neither is lying. They are quoting different clocks, and unless one of them tells you which, you cannot know which applies to you.

Almost every fixture list is published in the **local time of the country hosting the match**, or in the local time of the publisher — and those are frequently not the same thing. This guide covers how to read one correctly, and the specific weeks each year when the arithmetic everyone relies on quietly stops working.

## Fixture times are local to somewhere, and the label matters

A Premier League fixture at "17:30" means 17:30 in Britain. A La Liga fixture at "21:00" means 21:00 in Spain. Neither statement tells a reader in Toronto, Lagos or Sydney anything useful until it is anchored to a named zone.

Three labels do most of the work in European football:

- **GMT** — Greenwich Mean Time, Britain's winter clock, identical to UTC+0
- **BST** — British Summer Time, Britain's clock from late March to late October, UTC+1
- **CET / CEST** — Central European Time and its summer form, covering most of continental Europe at UTC+1 and UTC+2 respectively

The most common error in published listings is treating **GMT and BST as interchangeable**. They differ by a full hour, and Britain spends about seven months of the year on BST. A schedule that says "GMT" all year round is wrong from late March to late October — and it is wrong in the direction that makes people miss kick-off.

## Why the "usual" time difference is wrong twice a year

Here is the trap that catches even careful readers.

Most people carry a rule of thumb: London is five hours ahead of New York, one hour behind Berlin, and so on. That rule holds for most of the year and then breaks, because **countries do not change their clocks on the same date**.

- The United States switches on the **second Sunday in March** and back on the **first Sunday in November**
- The European Union switches on the **last Sunday in March** and back on the **last Sunday in October**

In 2026 that produces two windows where the familiar offset is simply wrong:

| Period | What happens | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 8 March – 29 March 2026 | US on summer time, Europe still on winter time | Europe-to-US gap is **one hour smaller** than usual for three weeks |
| 25 October – 1 November 2026 | Europe back on winter time, US still on summer time | Gap is **one hour smaller** than usual for one week |

The spring gap is the dangerous one. It lasts three weeks, it covers a busy part of the European season, and anyone converting a kick-off time using their year-round mental arithmetic will be an hour out for the whole of it.

The southern hemisphere inverts this again — Australia and New Zealand shift in the opposite direction to Europe — so the Europe-to-Sydney offset moves several times a year.

## How to convert a kick-off time correctly

The reliable method is short.

1. **Find the zone the time is quoted in.** If a listing does not say, treat the time as unverified rather than assuming.
2. **Convert through UTC, not through a remembered offset.** UTC does not observe daylight saving, so it is the one fixed point in the system.
3. **Check the date, not just the time.** A 20:00 kick-off in Europe is the following morning in much of Asia and Oceania. The day of the week changes, and people miss matches by a day rather than an hour.
4. **Re-check anything scheduled near a clock change.** Late March, late October and early November are when stored conversions go stale.

## Why we show times differently

A kick-off time rendered in one fixed timezone is wrong for almost everyone reading it. A site that prints every match in British time is answering a question only British visitors asked.

So we do not do that. Times on this site are rendered in **your own timezone**, taken from your device, with the zone abbreviation generated rather than hard-coded — because a fixed label like "BST" is wrong for five months of the year even in Britain.

Every timestamp also carries a machine-readable value in the underlying markup, so calendar tools, search engines and assistive technology read an unambiguous instant rather than a piece of text that needs interpreting.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does BST mean on a football schedule?**
British Summer Time, UTC+1, in force from the last Sunday in March to the last Sunday in October. Outside those dates Britain is on GMT, UTC+0.

**Why do two sites show different kick-off times for the same match?**
Almost always because they are quoting different local clocks — one the host country's, one the publisher's — and at least one of them has not labelled which.

**Is UTC the same as GMT?**
For reading a fixture list, treat them as the same. UTC is a precise time standard and GMT is a timezone that happens to sit at UTC+0; the technical distinction does not affect kick-off times.

**When do the clocks change in 2026?**
The US moved to summer time on 8 March and returns on 1 November. The EU moved on 29 March and returns on 25 October.

**Why does the date change for some matches?**
Because a European evening kick-off falls after midnight in much of Asia and Oceania. Always read the date alongside the time.
