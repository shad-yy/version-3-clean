# Why the Saturday 3pm Kick-Off Isn't on TV in Britain (But Is Almost Everywhere Else)

Saturday 3pm matches are not shown live on British television because England and Scotland are the only football associations in Europe that still use UEFA's "blocked hours" provision. It blocks live broadcasting between roughly 2.45pm and 5.15pm on Saturdays. The rule is territorial, not universal: in most other countries, those same fixtures are broadcast normally.

That distinction matters more than it first appears. Almost every explanation of the blackout treats it as a fact of football. It is not. It is a choice one or two national associations make, and the rest of Europe has stopped making it.

## What UEFA Article 48 actually does

Article 48 of the UEFA Statutes gives each national association the right to designate a **two-and-a-half-hour window**, on a Saturday or a Sunday, during which live football may not be broadcast within its territory. UEFA calls these "blocked broadcasting hours."

Two points are commonly misreported:

- **UEFA does not impose the blackout.** It permits one. The decision to use it belongs to each national association.
- **It is a window, not a fixture list.** The rule blocks a period of time. Any match falling inside it is affected, wherever in the world that match is being played.

According to UEFA's published blocked-hours documentation, England and Scotland both declare a Saturday window of 15:45–18:15 CET, which is 14:45–17:15 in British time.

## Who still uses it

This is the part that gets left out. Of all UEFA's member associations, only the English and Scottish associations currently exercise the option. The United Kingdom is the last significant holdout in Europe.

The practical consequence: a Premier League match kicking off at 3pm on a Saturday can be watched live in the large majority of countries that hold rights to it. It simply cannot be watched live in the country where it is being played.

If you have ever been abroad on a Saturday afternoon and found a match on television that you could not have watched at home, that is why. Nothing unusual happened. You crossed a rights boundary.

## Why it exists at all

The blackout dates to the 1960s. Football League chairmen took the view that televising matches at the traditional Saturday-afternoon kick-off time would reduce attendance at every other ground playing at the same hour. Lower divisions and non-league clubs, whose gate receipts matter proportionally far more than a top-flight club's, were the intended beneficiaries.

Whether the protection still works as designed is genuinely contested. The original reasoning predates the internet, pay television, and a Premier League fixture list scattered across five kick-off slots. What is not contested is that the rule is doing something narrower than most people assume: it protects the 3pm slot specifically, and only inside Britain.

## What this means when you are looking for a match

Three things follow that are worth knowing.

**A missing broadcast is not always a missing broadcast.** If a fixture appears in the schedule but no channel is listed for your country, the fixture is still being played and is very likely being shown somewhere. Availability is a property of your location, not of the match.

**Kick-off time determines everything.** The blackout is keyed to the clock, not the competition. Move the same fixture to 12:30 or 17:30 and it becomes televisable in Britain. This is precisely why so many matches are scheduled outside the 3pm slot.

**Highlights are unaffected.** The restriction covers live broadcasting during the blocked window. Recorded highlights after it closes are a separate rights package, which is why they have been a fixture of British television for decades.

## The wider point about where to watch

The 3pm blackout is the clearest example of something that shapes every answer to "where can I watch this": **broadcast rights are sold territory by territory, and the answer changes at every border.**

There is no single global channel for a competition. There is a different rights holder in each market, sometimes several, with different packages, different platforms, and — as the blackout shows — sometimes different rules about whether a match may be shown at all.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the 3pm blackout a law?**
No. It is a rule enforced under Article 48 of the UEFA Statutes, adopted at the discretion of a national association. It is not statute.

**Which hours exactly are blocked?**
UEFA's documentation records a Saturday window of 15:45–18:15 CET for both England and Scotland, equivalent to 14:45–17:15 UK time.

**Do other European countries have a blackout?**
Article 48 is available to every UEFA member association, but only the English and Scottish associations currently use it.

**Why are some Saturday matches on television in Britain, then?**
Because they kick off outside the blocked window. A 12:30 or 17:30 Saturday kick-off is not affected.

**Was the blackout ever suspended?**
Yes. It was lifted temporarily during the 2019-20 season when matches were played without crowds, which removed the attendance rationale it exists to protect. It was subsequently reinstated.
